# The comic threads of the Wine-Dark Sea

[Read the full poem plan and chapter map](README.md).

Research and original quest design for all eleven Aristophanes records in the existing pack. Exact work and place IDs come from `v2/content/greek-drama/card-world.json`.

## Shared shape

Each chapter has three short quest cards. Their first lines invite an action. The player then chooses the chapter's turn. Its chosen closing couplet enters the growing poem. The labels, quests, branches, consequences and verse below are new CosyWorld writing. Each **Source ground** paragraph identifies the play material that supports the design.

Comic chapters should change the larger poem. A supper becomes public spending. A private truce becomes a border. A refuge grows a throne. The dead poets argue over the very song the player has made. The chorus may laugh at the player's self-image and may repeat an earlier proud line with one changed word.

Lysistrata offers a useful central image: she explains civic repair through wool work, including loose threads brought into one cloth. That image can join Dione's household work to the whole world. Source location: the debate with the Magistrate, after her account of carding wool, where she compares the colonies to separate hanks gathered into a public tunic. [Primary text, Lysistrata: the scattered threads](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8688/pg8688.html#:~:text=Then%20as%20for%20our%20Colonies)

All branches below are adaptation choices. A branch marked **source-shaped** follows the broad movement of the play. The other branches are new possible turns. Those labels belong in the design notes; player cards carry the verse.

## Time and story frame

All eleven work records belong to `civic-athens`. Present these as the civic comic age, reached through the voyage and introduced by its chorus. Earlier mythic events can arrive as songs, witnesses in a retelling, staged scenes, or remembered objects. Each such return names its frame. *Frogs* opens an underworld comic journey in which dead poets can argue over those stories. The loom joins consequences and themes across these frames.

## 1. Acharnians — A peace small enough to drink

**Work:** `aristophanes-acharnians`\
**Place:** `greek-acharnae` — Acharnae Market

**Source ground:** Dicaeopolis secures a private truce. Angry charcoal burners challenge him. His market admits traders from enemy lands. His feast sits beside Lamachus's return from military duty with an injury. The play makes private pleasure and public war share the stage. [The Acharnians](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/acharnians.html)

**Conflict:** How far should one household's peace reach?

**Quest cards:**

1. **Three jars of peace.** “Three jars wait in the dust. / Taste the years inside them.” Choose a truce and carry its terms through the hostile market.
   Earned line: “I carried promised quiet years inside a jar.”
2. **The charcoal oath.** “Black hands circle my little peace. / Let each burned field speak.” Hear a ruined farmer, a trader and Dicaeopolis. Set the market boundary in public.
   Earned line: “The market stones grew warm beneath our hands.”
3. **Two suppers.** “Wine foams beside a soldier's bandage. / Whose bowl shall I fill?” Host the first meal under the chosen terms.
   Earned line: “The first bread broke beneath the eyes of war.”

**Closing branches:**

- **Private feast — source-shaped.** Keep the truce for the household. Trade thrives inside its stones; displaced farmers demand access later. “I drank my peace from a private cup. / Beyond the stones, the shields still rang.”
- **Shared market.** Spend part of the surplus on a truce lane for neighbors. Fewer goods remain for the feast. Traders become witnesses for *Lysistrata*. “We moved the stones beyond our door. / Peace smelled of onions, sweat, and rain.”
- **Soldier's table.** Use the truce to feed the wounded from both sides. Commanders place the market under watch. “I set two enemies beside the bread. / Their crutches leaned against one wall.”

**Threads carried:** market access, a veteran's testimony, the private cup. Echo Dione's table. Let *Peace* ask whether a full bowl can survive a war economy.

## 2. Knights — The mouth that owns the city

**Work:** `aristophanes-knights`\
**Place:** `greek-demos-house` — House of Demos

**Source ground:** A servant and a sausage seller compete for Demos through food, flattery, insults and rival claims. Public power appears as a household whose master can be pleased, cheated and won. [The Knights](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/knights.html)

**Conflict:** Who feeds the people, and who feeds upon them?

**Quest cards:**

1. **The stolen dish.** “A fine speech steams beneath the lid. / Lift it. Count the mouths it feeds.” Follow a meal from kitchen to master's table; mark who paid and who claimed credit.
   Earned line: “I lifted praise and found a borrowed meal.”
2. **The sausage oracle.** “The oracle smells of garlic. / Read the grease between its lines.” Assemble both rivals' promises and test one against the household accounts.
   Earned line: “The greasy page gave up its little god.”
3. **The master's ring.** “A ring lies warm between two palms. / Give its weight a living hand.” Present the evidence at a feast and choose who controls the stores.
   Earned line, one steward: “Our supper placed a city in one hand.” Shared keepers: “Our supper placed the keys among its keepers.”

**Closing branches:**

- **Crown the rival — source-shaped.** Help the sausage seller win. A new steward owes the player favors; the public still depends on a single favorite. “The sausage took the silver seal. / The city licked its greasy thumb.”
- **Open the stores.** Give Demos a public count and a rotating group of keepers. Rivals turn their insults toward the player. “We hung the keys beside the bowls. / Each hungry mouth could count the grain.”
- **Sell the victory.** Trade the deciding evidence for supplies for the voyage. The new steward sends generous gifts and expects flattering verses. “My sail grew fat on kitchen gold. / Behind me, louder bellies cheered.”

**Threads carried:** treasury trust, a patron's claim on the poem, grain accounts. Feed into *Assemblywomen*, *Wealth* and the poet trial in *Frogs*.

## 3. Clouds — A ladder made of words

**Work:** `aristophanes-clouds`\
**Place:** `greek-thinkery` — The Thinkery

**Source ground:** Strepsiades seeks arguments that will evade debts created by his son's costly habits. His son's new skill later defends violence against his father. The play ends with an attack on the school. Its Socrates is a comic stage figure. [The Clouds](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/clouds.html)

**Conflict:** Does a clever tongue owe anything to the people it hurts?

**Quest cards:**

1. **A debt beneath the bed.** “The old man's blanket rustles with bills. / Name the horses in his sleep.” Read the debt tablets with father, son and creditor.
   Earned line: “The horses slept; their hunger filled the room.”
2. **The crooked ladder.** “A word can climb a wall of stone. / Whose roof will bear its weight?” Argue both sides of one real claim before witnesses.
   Earned line: “I watched a clever word take human weight.”
3. **The torch at school.** “A torch waits where the lesson ends. / Choose what leaves this room alive.” Resolve the quarrel after the son's argument turns against his father.
   Earned line: “The lesson followed us beyond the door.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The clever escape.** Win the debt case through a false account. The son later uses the same reasoning to claim the father's goods. “I bent the truth around a coin. / The bent thing came to shape our house.”
- **The spoken debt.** Negotiate payment and make the family answer for its violence. A creditor joins the witnesses at later trials. “We set the debt upon the table. / Each honest word cost us a horse.”
- **The burning lesson — source-shaped.** Aid the attack on the school. The debt remains; teachers scatter with stories of the player. “We fed our shame to hungry flame. / By dawn, the ashes knew our names.”

**Threads carried:** truthful witness, persuasive deceit, destroyed learning. Reprise in *Ion*, *Helen* and *Eumenides*: a skill that wins an argument may distort a life.

## 4. Wasps — The sting in the voting jar

**Work:** `aristophanes-wasps`\
**Place:** `greek-jury-house` — The Juror's House

**Source ground:** Philocleon loves jury service. His son confines him, then stages a household trial of a dog accused of taking cheese. The father's later social freedom brings fresh harm and complaints. [The Wasps](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/wasps.html); [complete text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8689/pg8689.html)

**Conflict:** What becomes of a judge when judgment is also his wage, pleasure and pride?

**Quest cards:**

1. **Smoke with sandals.** “An old man climbs the chimney. / His jury token knocks against the bricks.” Help father and son set terms for the day's hearing.
   Earned line: “His sandals tapped against the chimney wall.”
2. **The cheese on trial.** “The dog has eaten all the evidence. / Bring the plate before the court.” Stage the mock trial; choose witnesses and reveal each hidden interest.
   Earned line: “A plate stood witness to the missing cheese.”
3. **The baker's broken tray.** “A freed judge dances through the bread. / Gather the price of his delight.” Hear those harmed during the revel and settle their claims.
   Earned line: “The broken tray lay quiet after laughter.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The tricked acquittal — source-shaped first turn.** Help the son manipulate the vote; pay compensation after the revel. The father resents the trick while the injured receive repair. “We stole one sting from the old wasp. / His dance still broke the baker's tray.”
- **The fair hearing.** Keep the vote clear and let the father judge the evidence. He gains a useful place in later public hearings. “The pebble fell where reason led. / A little cheese survived the law.”
- **The judge for hire.** Back the father's faction in exchange for a future verdict. Another claimant remembers the bargain. “I bought a sting and called it law. / The hive remembered where I stood.”

**Threads carried:** trial custom, juror allegiance, compensation. Directly challenge the court founded in *Eumenides*. The same voting pebble can hold mercy or purchased power.

## 5. Peace — The beetle bears the farmer

**Work:** `aristophanes-peace`\
**Place:** `greek-divine-heights` — The Divine Heights

**Source ground:** Trygaeus flies toward the gods on a dung beetle. Peace must be released through shared effort. The return of farm life changes the fortunes of people who sell the tools of war. [Peace](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/peace.html)

**Conflict:** Who will carry peace, and who will pay for its return?

**Quest cards:**

1. **A saddle full of heaven.** “Feed the beetle. Tighten the straps. / The gods shall smell our coming.” Prepare the absurd ascent with the farm household.
   Earned line: “We rose on wings that smelled of earth.”
2. **The rope of many hands.** “Peace lies beneath a stone. / Give every hand a place upon the rope.” Recruit groups with clashing interests; choose the terms that keep them pulling.
   Earned line: “The rope drew tight across our different hands.”
3. **A helmet full of beans.** “The smith brings helmets to the feast. / Find a use that leaves them warm.” Arrange work, food and tools for the first season after the rescue.
   Earned line: “A helmet waited for its second use.”

**Closing branches:**

- **Harvest feast — source-shaped.** Free Peace and favor the farmers. War sellers lose their trade and become a loud opposition. “The beetle bowed beneath the sky. / We sowed the field where spears had stood.”
- **A living for the smiths.** Free Peace and turn war workshops toward farm tools and vessels. The first harvest pays for the change. “We paid the smith to shape a plough. / His hammer kept the peace awake.”
- **Peace for a price.** Bargain access to Peace through a private gate. Rich households prosper; others carry petitions to the player. “We freed the goddess, priced the gate. / Her shadow crossed the hungry fields.”

**Threads carried:** harvest, workshop work, public or private access. Receives *Acharnians* and *Lysistrata*; feeds *Wealth*. A saved harvest can answer a hungry chorus in a tragedy.

## 6. Birds — The sky learns borders

**Work:** `aristophanes-birds`\
**Place:** `greek-bird-wilderness` — The Hoopoe's Thicket

**Source ground:** Travelers build a city among birds. Its control of the air gives it power over gods and people. The ruler's growing authority includes violent punishment of birds. The extravagant victory holds a sharp question about power. [The Birds](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/birds.html); [complete text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8689/pg8689.html)

**Conflict:** Can a refuge stay welcoming once it controls the road through heaven?

**Quest cards:**

1. **The hoopoe's invitation.** “Two weary men ask wings for shelter. / Hear the nests beneath their dream.” Ask the birds what their new city should protect.
   Earned line: “The nests heard out the travelers beneath them.”
2. **The wall across the wind.** “We raise a wall upon the air. / Who owns the smoke that climbs it?” Set passage rules for migrants, prayers and divine messengers.
   Earned line: “Our wall cast shadows on the paths of prayers.”
3. **The crown and the cooking pot.** “A crown shines beside a covered pot. / Ask which birds paid for the feast.” Expose the cost of the ruler's triumph and choose the city's law.
   Earned line: “The covered pot grew heavy beside the crown.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The sky throne — source-shaped.** Back the ruler's claim to divine power. He grants fast passage to allies and charges others. “We built a refuge in the blue. / A crown grew heavy on its wings.”
- **The open flight.** Give the birds shared rule and free corridors. Defenses stay weaker; migration carries aid and news across the map. “We left a doorway in the wind. / Small wings brought news from every shore.”
- **The broken wall.** Help threatened birds leave and pull down the air barrier. The city loses its grand promise; a living flock follows the ship. “We broke the gate that claimed the sky. / Our roof became a thousand wings.”

**Threads carried:** passage, refuge, divine pressure, ruler allegiance. Links to the asylum pleas in *Suppliants*, the chains in *Prometheus Bound*, and the disputed authority of *Bacchae*.

## 7. Lysistrata — The city on the loom

**Work:** `aristophanes-lysistrata`\
**Place:** `greek-acropolis` — Acropolis Treasury

**Source ground:** Women from opposing communities use a sexual strike and control of the treasury to force a settlement of the war. Lysistrata compares the work of governing to preparing wool and drawing scattered threads into cloth. [Lysistrata, full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8688/pg8688.html)

**Conflict:** Can a coalition turn its shared pressure into peace that lasts beyond the feast?

**Quest cards:**

1. **The oath beside the cup.** “Our beds grow cold; the wine grows warm. / Speak the vow each heart can keep.” Help each adult participant state their chosen pledge and household needs.
   Earned line: “The cup passed slowly through our separate vows.”
2. **The treasury shuttle.** “Silver sleeps behind the bar. / Draw its thread toward living hands.” Keep the treasury occupation supplied and audit the war spending.
   Earned line: “We counted silver in the shuttered room.”
3. **The cloth of scattered towns.** “Bring every loose thread to the light. / Let the treaty hold their names.” Carry terms between sides and decide who can speak at the settlement.
   Earned line: “The scattered threads lay bright across our knees.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The feast of return — source-shaped.** Secure a settlement and return the keys to the city's old keepers. Homes reopen; the coalition's future influence rests on its public promise. “The bolts drew back; the lamps burned late. / Two cities learned one drunken tune.”
- **The lasting council.** Keep a mixed council over war spending as part of the peace. Old officials challenge its power. “We wove our names into the peace. / The keys lay bright among the threads.”
- **The narrow treaty.** Accept favorable terms for Athens and leave allies' claims open. The feast succeeds while the coalition breaks into competing songs. “Our city kissed the cup of peace. / Across the sea, another cup lay dry.”

**Threads carried:** coalition strength, treaty terms, treasury rule. Echoes *Antigone*: a household voice becomes a public act. Receives testimony from *Trojan Women* and *Acharnians*. Creates the strongest literal loom scene.

## 8. Women at the Thesmophoria — Those named by another mouth

**Work:** `aristophanes-women-at-the-thesmophoria`\
**Place:** `greek-festival` — The Women's Festival

**Source ground:** Festival women challenge Euripides's portrayals of them. His disguised relative enters their gathering and is exposed. Rescue efforts parody tragic scenes. The play makes an audience answer the poet who depicts it. [The Thesmophoriazusae](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/thesmoph.html)

**Conflict:** Who gets to shape the public story of another person's life?

**Quest cards:**

1. **Names beneath the garlands.** “The poet's women gather at the gate. / Hear the lives between his lines.” Collect three speakers' accounts and let each choose what may be shared.
   Earned line: “The women laid their names beneath the flowers.”
2. **The borrowed dress.** “A borrowed hem catches on the door. / Ask the guest whose tale he serves.” Deal with the disguised visitor through a hearing, a ruse or an escape.
   Earned line: “A borrowed dress brought hidden speech to light.”
3. **The scene returned.** “The painted queen steps from her frame. / Give her a line the poet must hear.” Stage the disputed story before the festival chorus.
   Earned line: “The stage grew wide enough to hold an answer.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The comic rescue — source-shaped.** Aid the poet's staged rescue of his relative. The escape succeeds; the women keep a sharp counter-song about his tricks. “We slipped his kin through borrowed scenes. / The women kept the final laugh.”
- **The answered poem.** Broker a performance with the women's own replies. A named speaker can later answer the player's epic. “She took her name from out his mouth. / The whole hall learned another tune.”
- **The festival judgment.** Expose the visitor and let the assembly set terms for his release. The poet contests the judgment in public. “We held the mask before the crowd. / Its painted lips grew still at dawn.”

**Threads carried:** permission to tell a story, credited witnesses, counter-chorus. Revisit *Medea*, *Helen* and *Hippolytus* through their differing speakers. The completed poem can retain two voices beside one event.

## 9. Frogs — The song that returns

**Work:** `aristophanes-frogs`\
**Place:** `greek-underworld` — The Underworld Landing

**Source ground:** Dionysus and Xanthias travel below. A contest sets Aeschylus and Euripides against one another over poetry and the city's needs. Dionysus chooses Aeschylus for the return. [The Frogs](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/frogs.html); [complete text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8689/pg8689.html)

**Conflict:** What kind of song can serve a city after so much suffering?

**Quest cards:**

1. **The pack and the lion skin.** “A god wears courage over fear. / Take one burden from the servant.” Cross with Dionysus and Xanthias; choose who bears luggage, blame and public credit.
   Earned line, carrying the load: “I felt the servant's burden through the strap.” Leaving it with Xanthias: “I watched the servant bear our load below.”
2. **The weighing of the lines.** “Lay our verses on the scales. / Let the dead pronounce their weight.” Offer actual earned stanzas to the poets. Survivors' replies become evidence.
   Earned line: “Our verses shook the balance in the dark.”
3. **One voice for the upper air.** “The boat can carry home a song. / Choose what the waking city hears.” Set the return performance and its answering chorus.
   Earned line: “The boat turned toward the world that made our song.”

**Closing branches:**

- **Aeschylus returns — source-shaped.** Choose the voice of duty and common judgment. The return song draws on shared oaths; individual losses answer it in the margins. “We raised an oak from out the dark. / Its roots held fast around our dead.”
- **Euripides returns.** Choose a voice that presses private pain into public argument. The song reopens old settlements through their human costs. “We brought a lamp from under earth. / Each hidden face stepped through its flame.”
- **The living chorus.** Leave both poets their contest and carry the earned voices home with Xanthias as a named singer. The closing poem has several speakers. “We hauled our song across the reeds. / The servant took the leading breath.”

**Threads carried:** who speaks the final poem, whose work receives credit, how earlier choices are judged. This is a late convergence chapter. It must use the player's actual history. A traveler with few chapters can return with a small song; a long journey yields more voices and sharper disputes.

## 10. Assemblywomen — A feast with a law beside it

**Work:** `aristophanes-assemblywomen`\
**Place:** `greek-assembly` — The Assembly Hill

**Source ground:** Praxagora's group takes control of the assembly in disguise. Their plan shares property and food, then also imposes rules on sexual access. The play lets a grand scheme collide with evasion, appetite and coercion. [The Ecclesiazusae](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/eccles.html)

**Conflict:** Where does shared care become control over another person's life?

**Quest cards:**

1. **Beards before sunrise.** “Borrowed beards brush borrowed cloaks. / Rehearse the voice that wins the hill.” Prepare the takeover and choose whose interests the speech names.
   Earned line: “At dawn, the borrowed beards began to speak.”
2. **The cart of common goods.** “Bring your bright pot to the square. / Count the carts that turn for home.” Audit contributions and hear those who hide goods or fear losing them.
   Earned line: “The carts rolled slowly past the waiting bowls.”
3. **The feast and the decree.** “The feast has room for every bowl. / Read the law beside each bed.” Decide how the new rule treats food, private bonds and bodily choice.
   Earned line: “We read the law beside the steaming feast.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The whole decree — source-shaped.** Support the broad scheme, including enforced intimate duties. The feast grows lavish; affected people demand release from the law. “We shared the bread, then claimed the bed. / The feast rang louder than their cries.”
- **The common table.** Share stores and meals while each person keeps control of their body and bonds. The scheme costs more and needs steady public work. “We set one table in the square. / Each body kept its own front door.”
- **The hidden pot.** Help a household conceal its wealth while taking a public share. The player gains supplies; a later shortage draws scrutiny. “My secret pot stayed full at home. / I praised the feast with borrowed teeth.”

**Threads carried:** common stores, private trust, consent, evaded duties. Connect Dione's table to *Wealth*. Let *Lysistrata's* coalition argue over the new decree.

## 11. Wealth — The god who sees the bowl

**Work:** `aristophanes-wealth`\
**Place:** `greek-attic-farm` — The Attic Farm

**Source ground:** Chremylus meets the blind god Wealth and pursues a cure. Poverty argues that labor and wealth need each other. The change in who receives riches also changes claims, worship and dependence. [Plutus](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristophanes/plutus.html)

**Conflict:** Who should prosperity serve, and whose hands sustain it?

**Quest cards:**

1. **A god at the field gate.** “A blind god asks the road to dinner. / Take his hand across the furrows.” Learn where wealth has gone and whom it has passed by.
   Earned line: “A god took my hand beside the broken plough.”
2. **Poverty's sharp tongue.** “Poverty stands beside the sickle. / Hear the wage beneath her words.” Hear workers, householders and Poverty before arranging the cure.
   Earned line: “The sickle shone between our words of wealth.”
3. **The first clear morning.** “The god can see each waiting bowl. / Choose the hands that fill them.” Set the first distribution and the work that will sustain it.
   Earned line: “The morning found our choices in each bowl.”

**Closing branches:**

- **The worthy feast — source-shaped aim.** Restore sight and favor those judged worthy. New gatekeepers argue over who deserves a share. “The god saw virtue through our eyes. / Our friends grew bright beneath his gaze.”
- **The living share.** Give each household a basic share and pay the people who grow and carry food. This spends the first great windfall on a lasting system. “We counted hands beside the grain. / The harvest learned each worker's name.”
- **The favored farm.** Direct the windfall to the farm and the player's allies. Dependents gather at the gate with praise and petitions. “Gold filled the furrows of our field. / The hungry learned our house by heart.”

**Threads carried:** who gets a share, paid labor, gatekeepers, patronage. This can close the food thread begun with Dione's water jar. Its final image should recall the exact first table the player helped, used, or left.

## How these eleven chapters enter the wider poem

Use three loose braids. The player can enter them through earlier tragic choices.

1. **Bread and power:** Dione → *Acharnians* → *Knights* / *Assemblywomen* → *Wealth*. The same bowl changes from welcome to property to public claim.
2. **Peace and passage:** burial and refuge stories → *Lysistrata* / *Peace* → *Birds*. Ask who gains access to a home, a city, the sea and the sky.
3. **Witness and song:** *Clouds* / *Wasps* → *Women at the Thesmophoria* → *Frogs*. Ask who can prove, judge and tell the events the player has caused.

These braids cross through named people and objects. A market witness can appear at a trial. A helmet becomes a bean pot. A hidden coin buys a poem. The player should see the cost of an earlier clever choice when its beneficiary or victim returns.

## Card and poem rules for the comic voice

- Keep the card to a title and two short lines. Put the immediate action in a small separate line when needed.
- Give every quest a concrete noun: cup, sausage, pebble, beetle, key, mask, pot.
- Earn one lasting verse at each meaningful turn. Ordinary travel can supply a short link between verses.
- Let the chorus answer the traveler. The answer may puncture a proud claim through a visible fact.
- Keep the civic argument inside the joke. The meal, body, wage and door carry the ideas.
- Keep a speaker's original witness beside a later reply. The poem gains tension through the two voices.
- Use *Frogs* to judge the actual poem. Its contest can quote a short earned line, then ask who paid for it.

## Example of one connected comic passage

This is a new sample path: shared market, lasting peace council, open flight, living share, living chorus.

> We moved the stones beyond our door.\
> Peace smelled of onions, sweat, and rain.\
> We wove our names into the peace.\
> The keys lay bright among the threads.\
> We left a doorway in the wind.\
> Small wings brought news from every shore.\
> We counted hands beside the grain.\
> The harvest learned each worker's name.\
> We hauled our song across the reeds.\
> The servant took the leading breath.

Each couplet records a particular choice. Another path can place the private cup beside the sky throne and make the final poet ask how a refuge became an empire.
